# Short-form (30–60 s) hooks, retention curves, and story templates for animated explainers

Context: a 2D cartoon explainer page (owl narrator + young guy character) covering money, how things work, and the body, for a US audience.

How much to trust this: almost all creator-educator material here comes through secondhand summaries of interviews and podcasts, not primary transcripts. Most retention benchmark numbers come from third-party blogs, and YouTube publishes no official thresholds. The best quantitative primary source found is Paddy Galloway's 2023 study of 5,400 Shorts. The best-controlled study of hook wording is OverseerOS (2025–26), and it only looks at videos that succeeded. Confidence is marked on each claim.

## Hook clarity vs confusion: giving context in the first second so the curiosity gap is understood

### Takeaway
A working hook first tells the viewer what the video is about (a topic they recognize, or a benefit or pain point), then opens a gap with a contrast ("but…") and a surprising turn. If the viewer can't name the topic in the first 1–2 seconds, the curiosity reads as confusion. The first frame, the on-screen text, and the spoken line should all say the same thing. Right after the hook, a "foreshadow/bridge" line states the conflict and what the viewer will get by the end.

### Cited Findings
- Kallaway's 3-step hook: (1) a **context lean-in** that states the topic clearly so viewers can self-select, built on common ground, a benefit or pain point, a metaphor, or a surprising fact; (2) a **scroll-stop interjection** with a contrast word ("but," "however," "yet," e.g. "but get this"); (3) a **contrarian snapback** that goes the opposite way from the lean-in while staying on topic. His example, the Las Vegas Sphere: compare the screen to IMAX (familiar), then "but get this… the most impressive part is the audio." He says he doesn't want to trick non-target viewers into watching. (Medium confidence: transcript summary) — [Sozai transcript of Kallaway video](https://sozai.app/transcript/create-irresistible-hooks-blow-content/)
- Kallaway's bad vs good example. Bad: "you should be taking magnesium because it's one of 21 core building block minerals," which needs prior knowledge and gives no benefit. Good: "if you want better sleep you need to be taking magnesium," which leads with the benefit and names the subject second. — [Sozai/Kallaway](https://sozai.app/transcript/create-irresistible-hooks-blow-content/)
- Kallaway calls visual hooks "far more powerful" than spoken ones. He recommends 3–5 bold on-screen words that match the spoken hook, plus some motion that catches attention without overwhelming. Use short staccato sentences at the start and longer ones later. Give the first "hit of value" inside or right after the hook. He says short-form viewers decide within about 4 seconds. — [Sozai/Kallaway](https://sozai.app/transcript/create-irresistible-hooks-blow-content/)
- Visual, spoken, and text hooks should line up so viewers aren't confused, and a hook should plant one clear question about one subject (secondary summary). — [Recapio digest of Kallaway "How to Create a Killer Hook"](https://recapio.com/digest/how-to-create-a-killer-hook-impossible-to-skip-by-kallaway); [OpenTools summary](https://opentools.ai/youtube-summary/how-to-create-irresistible-hooks-and-blow-up-your-content)
- Jenny Hoyos: the hook should be visual, and the first frame should work like a thumbnail/title even without audio. Keep it to about 3 seconds or less. **Right after the hook, foreshadow**: say what's coming, what the conflict is, and what the viewer gets by the end. Example: a $1 homemade chicken sandwich vs a $6 Chick-fil-A sandwich. She will rewrite the whole concept to get a stronger hook. Power words she uses: "banned," "free," "one dollar," "secret," "cheap." (Medium-low confidence: newsletter and AI summaries of her podcasts) — [Marketing Examined playbook](https://marketing-examined.beehiiv.com/p/jenny-hoyos-shortform-video-playbook); [Knowt notes on Colin & Samir-style interview "Meet the YouTuber Who Solved Shorts"](https://knowt.com/note/e40546ff-6565-4b65-acd6-6d3d2717dabe/Meet-the-YouTuber-Who-Solved-Shorts-Jen)
- Paddy Galloway: "Treat your intro like a thumbnail"; make the first second punchy. — [Galloway Shorts study thread, Apr 2023](https://threadreaderapp.com/thread/1646898356419981315.html)
- Adam Mosseri (Instagram head, Oct 2024 video, repeated in 2025 coverage): "The first three seconds of a reel are super important." The best hooks use on-screen text and set expectations for what's coming. Coverage quotes him saying "Now, we call this the hook" and says on-screen text was his first suggestion. (Primary video not checked directly; the Inman page returned 503 when fetched, so this rests on search-snippet summaries.) — [Inman, Mar 7 2025](https://news.inman.com/2025/03/07/head-of-instagram-reveals-secrets-to-social-media-success/)
- Common causes of a weak "viewed" share: an unclear first frame, slow setup, hard-to-read text, abrupt or unclear audio, or a title that doesn't match the content. Fixes: clarify the first frame, state the intended viewer sooner, use more specific on-screen text. — [Creator Essentials glossary: Viewed vs Swiped Away](https://www.creatoressentials.com/glossary/viewed-vs-swiped-away/)
- Hook wording in million-view Shorts (OverseerOS study, 1,178 Shorts hooks, 367 channels, median date about Aug 2025): median hook length is about 11 words, slightly shorter than long-form. The most common first words are "this" (11.6%), "what" (10.8%), "I" (6.5%), and "if" (4.8%). Demonstrative openers ("this/these/that") stay more common in Shorts even with controls, which fits "point at the thing on screen" hooks. — [OverseerOS hooks study](https://www.overseeros.com/blog/youtube-shorts-hooks-study)

### Inferences
- **Clarity formula for this page:** [Familiar subject named in the first 3–5 words, shown on screen] + [contrast word] + [surprising claim or stake]. Examples:
  - Confusing: "It's been lying to you your whole life." (What is "it"? No subject on screen.)
  - Clear: on screen, a credit card with the text "Your credit score is lying." Owl VO: "Your credit score goes UP when you borrow… but paying cash can actually hurt it."
  - Confusing: "Nobody talks about this one weird thing." Clear: guy holding his stomach, text "Why your stomach growls." Owl: "Your stomach growls when you're hungry, right? Wrong — it growls all day. You just can't hear it when it's full."
- **The bridge line** (Hoyos's "foreshadow") should come within roughly the first 3–6 s. It turns a shock into a promise. Example: "Here's what actually happens to your $1 after you swipe a card — and who gets a cut." Without it, a shocking hook leaves the viewer asking whether the video is about anything they care about.
- For a cartoon page, the first frame does a lot of the work. Show the subject (dollar bill, heart, ATM) already in motion or in trouble, with the guy character reacting, so the frame reads with the sound off. Avoid logo or title cards at frame 0 (consistent with the retention-curve fixes in the next section).
- Use the guy character as the stand-in for the viewer's existing belief (the "lean-in": "I always pay my card off early to help my score"). The owl delivers the snapback. That puts Kallaway's structure into the two characters naturally.

### Gaps
- No primary YouTube or Instagram study gives the time (e.g. "1 s" vs "2 s") by which topic clarity must land. The "viewer must know within 1–2 s" figure is common creator wisdom, not a sourced fact. Kallaway's own number is about 4 s for the decision.
- No controlled study found that compares "confusing" vs "clear" curiosity hooks directly.

## Hook types and evidence (negative/warning, question, bold claim, number, POV, "what happens if", visual)

### Takeaway
There's no reliable causal evidence that any one hook *type* retains better. The best-controlled data shows question-hook rates in Shorts and long-form are about the same once you compare within the same channels. Pick hook type to fit the topic. What has the most consistent support is clarity, a visual first frame, alignment of text, VO, and visual, and benefit or stakes for the viewer.

### Cited Findings
- No controlled study comparing question vs statement hook retention was found. In OverseerOS's million-view sample, questions looked twice as common in Shorts pooled (23.4% vs 11.8%), but within the 74 channels making both formats the rates were nearly identical (10.8% vs 10.6%). Digits: 15.5% vs 14.5% within the same channels. "You" appeared in about 35–37% of Shorts hooks. The authors say the data can't show causation because only successful videos were sampled. — [OverseerOS](https://www.overseeros.com/blog/youtube-shorts-hooks-study)
- Usage (not performance) data: in 348,346 exported 2025 videos, question hooks were the most common intentional type (12.2%), versus 6.6% for bold statements. This is vendor data and measures prevalence, not retention. — [Revid.ai "Anatomy of a viral short 2026"](https://www.revid.ai/blog/the-anatomy-of-a-viral-short-2026)
- Hook formats Kallaway names: benefit-first ("if you want X, you need Y"); contrarian/negative ("don't do Y" plus payoff); mistake/list ("there are three massive mistakes people are making with their mortgage"); "anti-listicle"; and **"cult hopping,"** which means referencing a known brand, celebrity, or culture to make a complex idea feel familiar. — [Sozai/Kallaway](https://sozai.app/transcript/create-irresistible-hooks-blow-content/)
- Hoyos's power words ("banned," "free," "one dollar," "secret," "cheap") and her price-contrast hook ("$1 chicken sandwich"), i.e. a specific number plus a known brand. — [Marketing Examined](https://marketing-examined.beehiiv.com/p/jenny-hoyos-shortform-video-playbook)
- Self-reported or uncited claims such as "+42% retention for curiosity-gap hooks" and "questions create curiosity faster than statements" have no visible methodology. **Low confidence; don't rely on them.** — [dev.to "Hook Engineering"](https://dev.to/bulkdl/hook-engineering-the-science-of-the-first-3-seconds-in-short-form-video-2026-2jp9); [Velio](https://velio.co/?p=3085)

### Inferences
How each hook type fits this explainer page (judgment, built on the principles above):
| Hook type | Fits when | Example (owl/guy) | Pitfall |
|---|---|---|---|
| Negative/warning | Money mistakes, body myths | "Stop paying your credit card in full on the due date." | Must pay off a real, specific risk, or it reads as clickbait |
| Question | The question is already in viewers' heads | "Why do your fingers prune in water — but not your arms?" | Vague questions ("Ever wondered about money?") give no topic clarity |
| Bold claim / contrarian | A well-known belief is wrong | "Your stomach doesn't digest food. Your small intestine does." | The claim has to be defensible; snapback, not lie |
| Number/specific | Prices, odds, body stats | "Your body makes 2 million red blood cells every second." | Keep it to one striking number; don't stack figures |
| "What happens if…" | Body and physics scenarios | Guy: "What happens if I swallow gum?" Owl: "Let's follow it." | The scenario has to be visual and the answer has to come |
| POV | Relatable money moments | "POV: you just got your first paycheck and $300 is missing." | Must name a recognizable situation in the text |
| Visual hook | Every video, combined with one of the above | First frame: guy's wallet catching fire | A visual with no stated subject confuses |
| Cult hopping / known reference | Abstract topics | "Why Costco hot dogs are still $1.50" | The brand has to be widely known in the US |

### Gaps
- No platform-published or peer-reviewed data on retention by hook type for 2024–2026 was found.
- No sourced evidence was found on "POV" hooks specifically for explainers.

## Retention curves: shape, drops, causes, fixes, benchmarks

### Takeaway
A typical Shorts curve has a "cliff" at 0–3 s (the swipe decision), slow decay through the middle with dips at specific seconds, and a drop at the end unless the video loops. YouTube's "Viewed vs Swiped Away" ("How many chose to view" / "Stayed to watch") measures the hook. Average percentage viewed and the curve measure the story. The most-cited real dataset (Galloway, 2023) found Shorts under 60% viewed rarely performed, and the best performers sat at 70–90%. YouTube publishes no official thresholds.

### Cited Findings
**Metric definitions**
- "Viewed vs Swiped Away" is labelled "How many chose to view" in Studio (Analytics → Content → Shorts). The positive side, "Stayed to watch," is the percentage of times viewers stayed past the opening seconds. YouTube doesn't say how many seconds. It isn't a completion metric. — [Creator Essentials](https://www.creatoressentials.com/glossary/viewed-vs-swiped-away/); [Creator Essentials: Stayed to Watch](https://www.creatoressentials.com/glossary/stayed-to-watch/)
- Since **March 31, 2025**, a Shorts "view" counts the moment playback starts or replays. The older stricter count is now called "engaged views" (Advanced Mode). — [Creator Essentials](https://www.creatoressentials.com/glossary/viewed-vs-swiped-away/); [search summary of Shortimize/1of10 guides](https://1of10.com/blog/youtube-shorts-analytics/)
- YouTube publishes no universal good or bad cutoff, and no rule that distribution stops at a set percentage. Compare against your own Shorts of similar length, format, and topic. — [Creator Essentials](https://www.creatoressentials.com/glossary/viewed-vs-swiped-away/); [Bytecap evidence synthesis, Jul 2026](https://www.bytecap.io/research/youtube-shorts-retention-benchmarks)

**Benchmarks (with confidence)**
- **Galloway (Apr 2023; 3.3B views, 33 channels, 5,400 Shorts; "still early research"):** "shorts with under 60% VVSA rarely performed well"; "the best performing shorts were typically between 70 and 90%." Shorts with AVD over 50 s averaged 4.1M views. Most Shorts were 20–40 s, longer Shorts did better on average, and there were outliers at every length. Likes, shares, and comments showed no strong relationship with performance. (Medium-high confidence; dated pre-2024.) — [Galloway thread](https://threadreaderapp.com/thread/1646898356419981315.html)
- Third-party "viewed" targets vary: 70%+, 75–80%, or "70%+ viral, 50/50 average." (Low confidence, inconsistent.) — [Subscribr](https://subscribr.ai/youtube-strategy/youtube-shorts-analytics-metrics-growth); [Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/the-creators-guide-to-youtube-shorts-analytics); [ContentStudio](https://contentstudio.io/blog/youtube-shorts-analytics-guide)
- Hoyos is reported to aim for about 90% retention, with her own swipe-through/viewed figure reported as 85% or 92% depending on the summary. Summaries contradict each other. (Low confidence.) — [Marketing Examined](https://marketing-examined.beehiiv.com/p/jenny-hoyos-shortform-video-playbook); [Knowt notes](https://knowt.com/note/e40546ff-6565-4b65-acd6-6d3d2717dabe/Meet-the-YouTuber-Who-Solved-Shorts-Jen)
- Aibrify (Apr 2026, vendor blog, mostly uncited): roughly 30–50% leave at 0–3 s and another 15–25% at 3–10 s. AVD above about 80% of length is the "informal" expanded-reach threshold, and reach stalls below 60%. Educational/list Shorts reach 85–95%, talking-head 55–70%. Change visuals every 1.5–2 s (under 1.2 s reads as noise, over 2.5 s causes dips). 60–80% of Shorts start sound-off. **Low confidence: the author admits the thresholds aren't YouTube-published.** — [Aibrify retention curve playbook](https://aibrify.com/en/blog/youtube-shorts-retention-curve-playbook)
- The "~50% of Reels viewers drop in 3 s" figure often attributed to Mosseri is **not from Mosseri**. It's a third-party estimate. Instagram "Skip Rate" reporting is described by blogs but wasn't confirmed by an official source in this research. — [search summary incl. Opus/Inro posts](https://www.opus.pro/blog/instagram-reels-hook-formulas)

**Curve shapes → causes → fixes**
- *Cliff* (30–50% drop at 1–3 s): slow open, intro cards or logos, "hey guys." Fix: open on the most visually loaded frame, captions by second 1. *Hump* (above 60% mid, then decay): good mid payoff but a hard stop. Fix: loop ending. *Plateau* (flat above 70%, spike above 100% at second 1): a clean loop. Mid dips map to a specific second, often a static shot, a slow-reading text card, or a visual pause. — [Aibrify](https://aibrify.com/en/blog/youtube-shorts-retention-curve-playbook) (low-medium confidence)
- Replays count, so looping Shorts can show average percentage viewed above 100%. A spike at second 1 suggests the loop works. Avoid explicit outros, "thanks for watching," and end screens if you want loops. — [Opus retention graphs](https://www.opus.pro/blog/youtube-retention-graphs-explained); [Subscribr low watch time](https://subscribr.ai/youtube-strategy/youtube-shorts-low-watch-time-boost)
- Diagnostic matrix: **high viewed, low retention** means the opening works but the middle loses people (delayed payoff, repetition, weak answer, pacing, unnecessary length). **Low viewed, good retention** means the niche is served but the opening or targeting is too narrow (clarify the first frame, name the viewer). **Both low** means fix the opening first. Change one opening principle at a time. — [Creator Essentials](https://www.creatoressentials.com/glossary/viewed-vs-swiped-away/)
- Hoyos diagnoses with two metrics: swipe rate shows hook problems and retention shows story problems. She cuts dead time at the end. One summary reports cutting a single second lifted retention from 83% to 88% (anecdotal). End right after the hook's promise is delivered. — [search summary of Hoyos interview notes](https://knowt.com/note/e40546ff-6565-4b65-acd6-6d3d2717dabe/Meet-the-YouTuber-Who-Solved-Shorts-Jen); [Coconote notes](https://coconote.app/notes/f37d7f5b-9939-4a07-803e-8bbf9f30b5aa)
- Galloway warns against overreacting to small retention dips and favors fixing story structure over chasing metric noise (secondary summary). — [Pulse.bot "This Is Why Your Shorts FAIL"](https://www.pulse.bot/creator-economy/video/this-is-why-your-shorts-fail-d9584254-4b96-4ba2-8769-314731b083ae/)

### Inferences
Drop → likely cause → script/edit fix, for this page:
- **0–3 s cliff:** topic unclear, title card, slow owl greeting. → Start mid-action, with the subject named in text and VO by about 1 s. No "Hoot here!" intro.
- **3–10 s slide:** no bridge or stakes. → Add a foreshadow line saying why it matters to the viewer's money or body and what they'll know by the end.
- **Mid dips:** repetition (restating the hook), static explanation shots, or a stretch with no "but." → Every 4–8 s, add a new beat with a "but/so" turn and change the visual (new prop, zoom, or character reaction) about every 2 s.
- **Early-payoff cliff:** answer given at 10 s with nothing left. → Answer in layers (see Payoff timing).
- **End drop:** "follow for more," a recap, or an end card. → End on the final reveal or punchline. Write the last line so it flows back into the first.
- Under the post–March 2025 view counting, raw view counts are inflated. Judge hooks with "Stayed to watch"/engaged views, not views.

### Gaps
- No official YouTube Help Center page was retrieved in this research (searches returned only third-party pages). Definitions are as relayed by glossaries.
- No credible 2024–2026 large-sample benchmark for average percentage viewed for 30–60 s Shorts specifically. Galloway's 2023 data is the most credible but predates the March 2025 view-count change.
- No Creator Insider or official Instagram retention benchmark found.

## Story templates for 30–60 s explainers

### Takeaway
The best-supported skeleton for any template is: **Hook (clear subject plus contrast) → Foreshadow (conflict and what you'll get) → "but/therefore" escalating beats → Payoff on the emotional high → hard end or loop.** Galloway's framing is that a Short is "an intro with a conclusion." The named templates are different ways of filling that skeleton. Evidence for specific templates is mostly creator practice, not data.

### Cited Findings
- Hoyos's structure: Hook → Foreshadow (conflict and expected outcome) → transitions using "but/then" (or "but therefore") so each beat builds toward the payoff → End on the biggest emotion (funniest punchline, biggest twist). Viewers judge the video by the intensity of the final emotion. The goal is "satisfied, but surprised." — [Marketing Examined](https://marketing-examined.beehiiv.com/p/jenny-hoyos-shortform-video-playbook); "but therefore" wording per [Knowt notes](https://knowt.com/note/e40546ff-6565-4b65-acd6-6d3d2717dabe/Meet-the-YouTuber-Who-Solved-Shorts-Jen)
- Hoyos writes the hook and the last line first, then fills in the middle. — [search summary of Hoyos interview notes](https://coconote.app/notes/f37d7f5b-9939-4a07-803e-8bbf9f30b5aa)
- **Comparison/versus** works for her ($1 homemade vs $6 Chick-fil-A). The transitions show ingredients and a running price total, with a "But is that enough to beat Chick-fil-A?" checkpoint. — [Marketing Examined](https://marketing-examined.beehiiv.com/p/jenny-hoyos-shortform-video-playbook)
- Galloway: "I almost think Shorts as intros… except they need to have a conclusion." — [EssentiallySports quoting Galloway on Jon Youshaei's podcast](https://www.essentiallysports.com/esports-news-shortz-are-like-intros-that-need-to-have-secret-content-consultant-of-mrbeast-shares-the-formula-of-making-perfect-youtube-shortz)
- Kallaway's **list/mistakes** ("three massive mistakes…") and **contrarian** (myth-busting) formats, plus "cult hopping" for comparisons to known brands. He also uses a curiosity "domino effect" where each line should pull the viewer to the next. — [Sozai/Kallaway](https://sozai.app/transcript/create-irresistible-hooks-blow-content/)
- Educational/list Shorts are claimed to reach 85–95% retention vs 55–70% for talking-head (vendor blog, uncited; low confidence). — [Aibrify](https://aibrify.com/en/blog/youtube-shorts-retention-curve-playbook)

### Inferences
Template cards (structure, when to use, pitfalls). These come from my own synthesis of the skeleton above, not from sourced findings.
1. **"What happens if…"** (body/physics). Hook: guy about to do X. Owl: "Here's what happens in the next 60 seconds." Beats are timestamps or stages (0 s → 10 min → 1 day), each with a "but." Payoff: the surprising final stage. *Pitfall:* stages that are all the same intensity. Each stage has to escalate.
2. **POV** (money, relatable). Text: "POV: your paycheck is $300 less than you thought." Guy reacts, and the owl itemizes where it went (federal, FICA, state, 401k), with each line as a reveal. Payoff: the one item the viewer can change. *Pitfall:* a POV that isn't recognizable to US viewers.
3. **Countdown/list ("3 things…").** Hook names the count and the stake. Number on screen. **Save the strongest for last** and tease it ("#1 is the one banks hate"). *Pitfall:* front-loading the best item, or items that are too alike, which creates repetition dips.
4. **Before/after or transformation.** Show the "after" in frame 1 ("$10,000 from $5 a day?"), then rewind to show how. *Pitfall:* the after-image gives away the whole payoff. Keep a final twist (e.g. the role of compound interest only shows up at the end).
5. **Myth-busting.** The guy states the common belief (lean-in), the owl says "But…" (interjection), then gives the truth plus proof (snapback). Payoff: the proof visual. *Pitfall:* a myth that no one believes (low stakes), or a correction that's only semantic.
6. **Mystery/whodunit.** "Someone takes $3 every time you buy coffee with a card. Who?" Suspects get eliminated one by one (the bank? the store? Visa?), and the reveal is the interchange fee split. *Pitfall:* the answer never comes clearly. Name it plainly at the end.
7. **"How it's made / how it works."** Follow one object (a dollar, a sip of water, a sneeze) through its journey. The hook states the surprising end point. *Pitfall:* a flat step-by-step with no "but." Insert obstacles ("but your stomach acid would dissolve it — so…").
8. **Comparison/versus.** "$1 vs $1,000 ___" or "Your heart vs a mouse's heart." Use a running scoreboard or price on screen (Hoyos-style). Payoff: the unexpected winner. *Pitfall:* the obvious winner.
9. **Timeline ("In 1971…").** Hook pairs the date with a present-day consequence the viewer feels ("In 1971 one decision is why your rent is so high"). Never open on the date alone, because it gives no stake. Jump forward in 2–4 time beats. *Pitfall:* history lecture pacing. Tie each beat to "you."
10. **"The reason why…"** ("The reason gas prices end in 9/10 of a cent"). The hook asserts that a hidden reason exists, then the false obvious answer gets knocked down before the real one. *Pitfall:* reveals the reason in the hook.

### Gaps
- No data found comparing performance of these named templates for explainers.
- No primary analysis of top animated explainer Shorts channels (e.g. Zack D. Films–style "what happens if" animation) was found. Searches returned nothing substantive about why that format works.

## Payoff timing: when to answer, false payoffs, final twist, loops

### Takeaway
Deliver a small "first hit of value" early (in or right after the hook), hold the main answer until the last 15–25% of the runtime, and end the video almost immediately after the payoff. Both an answer delivered too early and an answer that never arrives hurt. A clean last line that feeds back into the first line can create rewatches, which show up as retention above 100%.

### Cited Findings
- Front-load value: give a first "hit of value" in or right after the hook. — [Sozai/Kallaway](https://sozai.app/transcript/create-irresistible-hooks-blow-content/)
- End right after delivering the hook's promise, and trim dead time at the end. Hoyos's reported 83% to 88% lift from cutting one second is anecdotal. — [Knowt notes](https://knowt.com/note/e40546ff-6565-4b65-acd6-6d3d2717dabe/Meet-the-YouTuber-Who-Solved-Shorts-Jen); [Coconote](https://coconote.app/notes/f37d7f5b-9939-4a07-803e-8bbf9f30b5aa)
- End on the emotional high (biggest twist or punchline); "satisfied, but surprised." — [Marketing Examined](https://marketing-examined.beehiiv.com/p/jenny-hoyos-shortform-video-playbook)
- Shorts must conclude: "intros… except they need to have a conclusion." — [EssentiallySports/Galloway](https://www.essentiallysports.com/esports-news-shortz-are-like-intros-that-need-to-have-secret-content-consultant-of-mrbeast-shares-the-formula-of-making-perfect-youtube-shortz)
- Loop design: match the last frame's composition, color, or motion to the first. Avoid outros and end screens. A spike above 100% at second 1 means the loop is working. A mid-video spike above 100% means viewers rewound a surprising moment. — [Aibrify](https://aibrify.com/en/blog/youtube-shorts-retention-curve-playbook); [Opus](https://www.opus.pro/blog/youtube-retention-graphs-explained)
- The "high viewed, low retention" pattern points to delayed payoff, weak answers, or repetition. — [Creator Essentials](https://www.creatoressentials.com/glossary/viewed-vs-swiped-away/)
- Rewatchability is a Hoyos goal (summary). — [search summary of Hoyos notes](https://knowt.com/note/e40546ff-6565-4b65-acd6-6d3d2717dabe/Meet-the-YouTuber-Who-Solved-Shorts-Jen)

### Inferences
- **Timing map for a 45 s explainer:** 0–2 s hook (subject plus contrast). 2–6 s bridge/foreshadow. About 6–12 s first mini-payoff (a fact that rewards staying but raises a bigger question). 12–35 s escalating beats, one "but/therefore" every 4–8 s, optionally with a **false payoff** ("So it's the bank? Nope.") around the midpoint to reset curiosity. About 35–42 s main answer. 42–45 s twist or punchline, then cut. No CTA card.
- **False payoffs** work only if the real answer beats them clearly. Using more than one in 45 s risks feeling like stalling.
- **Answer-never-comes:** a vague ending ("it's complicated") breaks the hook's promise and probably hurts trust and comments. Always say the answer in a single plain sentence on screen.
- **Loop lines for the two characters:** end with the guy repeating the opening question in a new light, or end mid-sentence into the hook. Example: last line "…which is exactly why" flows into the first line "your credit score drops when you pay off a card." Keep the end-frame background and pose the same as the first frame.
