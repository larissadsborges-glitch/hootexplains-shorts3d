## Verified facts
**Day 1 (0–24 h)**
- 17 h awake ≈ 0.05% blood alcohol; 24 h awake ≈ 0.10% (CDC/NIOSH). US drink-driving limit is 0.08% (so 24 h is past it).
  Source: [CDC NIOSH](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/niosh/emres/longhourstraining/impaired.html)
- At 24 h: slower reactions, poorer memory and attention, impaired judgment, irritability; stress hormones cortisol and adrenaline rise ("your body's attempt to compensate"), per Dr Michelle Drerup, Cleveland Clinic.
  Source: [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/how-long-can-you-go-without-sleep)
- One night without sleep raises ghrelin (the hunger hormone) and hunger (Schmid et al. 2008, PubMed 18564298). Healthline (reviewed by Meir Kryger MD) lists increased appetite.

**Day 2 (24–48 h)**
- 36 h: higher inflammatory markers in the blood; hormone imbalance; body temperature, mood and appetite fluctuate (Cleveland Clinic).
- 48 h: microsleeps become likely: involuntary lapses lasting up to 30 s that you may not remember (Cleveland Clinic). Dangerous when driving.
- 24–48 h: perceptual distortions, anxiety, irritability, depersonalisation, losing track of time (Waters et al. 2018 review, Frontiers in Psychiatry: 21 studies, 760 people).
  Source: [PubMed 30042701](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30042701/)
- Our maths: 60 mph = 88 ft/s, so 5 s ≈ 440 ft ≈ 134 m: longer than a US football field (360 ft including the end zones).

**Day 3 (48–72 h+)**
- 48–90 h: complex hallucinations and disordered thinking; by day 3, hallucinations in all three senses (seeing, hearing, feeling); after 72 h: delusions; the picture resembles acute psychosis (Waters 2018).
- 72 h: the urge to sleep can become uncontrollable; microsleeps longer and more frequent (Healthline, reviewed by Meir Kryger MD).
- Recovery: short-term effects go away after sleep (Cleveland Clinic); in the review, normal sleep resolved symptoms in many cases, "although not all".
- Randy Gardner, 17, stayed awake 11 days (264 h) in Dec 1963–Jan 1964 for a school science fair project; slept about 14 hours afterwards and said he felt "groggy, but not any groggier than a normal person". Guinness stopped tracking sleep deprivation records in 1996 because of the harm.
  Source: [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2024/01/28/1227217274/sleep-deprivation-record)
